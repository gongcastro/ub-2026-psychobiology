What is language?


Finite system of symbols (words) that can be combined using rules to make a virtually infinite number of distinct sentences.

Words (and other linguistic units) are combined in systematic ways, following rules we know as grammar.

These rules set the basis for recursivity: a given structure can embedd other structures, resulting in a generative (infinite) range of patterns.

Variability in speech


Inter-speaker differences: the same speech sound, syllable or word sounds different depending on many factors.

Age kids’ voice is different than adults’ voice in many dimensions (e.g., pitch, intonation)
Sex female voice has higher pitch than male voice
Accent variations in pronunciation (e.g., American vs. British English)
Dialect variations in grammar and vocabulary (e.g., different words to express the same content)


This does not stop us from identifying the same words and activating the same meanings!

Aphasia

Also known as acquired language impairment.

Aphasia



Aphasia: disorder that results from damage—usually from a stroke or traumatic brain injury—to areas of the brain that are responsible for language (NIH)


Why does psycholinguistics/cognitive science care about language impairments? Why not study healthy participants with normative use of language?


The first neuroanatomical mapping of language functions (speaking, understanding, reading, writing) came from clinical studies on patients with brain lesions and language impairments.


Main causes of aphasia

Acquired brain damage after stroke damage (most common), head injury, progressive neurological disorders (e.g., Alzheimer), brain tumor, encephalitis.


Damage is more frequently located on left side of the brain.


Can affect one or more communication modalities:

Comprehension Production
Spoken Speech comprehension Speech production
Written Reading Writing

Classification of aphasias

Damage localization is associated with different resulting patterns of language impairment.

Classification of aphasias based on fluency, comprehension, and repetition ability.

Broca’s aphasia



Paul Broca (1861)

  • Inability to properly produce any words other than “tan”.
  • Speech comprehension intact
  • Post-mortem examination of the patient’s brain: damage to the 3rd convolution of the left frontal lobe

This area is now known as Broca’s Area. Corresponds to Broadmann’s area BA 44/45

Broca’s aphasia



More generally, patients with Broca’s aphasia show key difficulties in speech production, with speech comprehension intact.

What can we learn from the function of Broca’s area?

Broca’s area involved in:

  • Motor programing of articulatory movements (together with supplementary motor area, basal ganglia and cerebellum)
  • Grammatical processing (activates when judging the grammaticality of a sentence)

Wernicke’s aphasia



Carl Wenicke (1874):

  • Difficulties in speech comprehension
  • Fluent speech, typical speech rate, intact grammatical abilities
  • Produced speech does not have meaning (word salad)
  • Post-mortem examination of the patient’s brain: left temporal lobe

This area is now known as Wernickes’s area. Corresponds to Broadmann’s area BA 22/24.

Conduction aphasia



Brookshire and McNeil (2014):

  • Deficit in spoken word repetition: patients fail to encode phonological information (inverted positions of syllables)
  • Speech comprehension relatively preseved
  • Patients are aware of the errors (unlike other forms of aphasia) but still have difficulty in correcting their speech
  • Lesion in the arcuate fasciculus, often after stroke

Anomic aphasia and Primary Progressive Aphasia (PPA)



Brookshire and McNeil (2014):

  • Difficulty in word selection of (nouns and verbs): failed word retrieval
  • Fluent speech
  • Comprehension preserved
  • Can be very specific: color anomia (deficit in retrieving words for color)
  • Neuroanatomical correlates unclear: Peri-sylvian area, generally result of progressive diseases

Global aphasia


  • Serious deficit of speech production and comprehension

  • Patients are almost mute or produced stereotyped sentences (formed by very few disconnected words)

  • Repetitions, reading, writing all compromised patients are able to express themselves using intonation, facial expression, gestures

  • Lesion at left inferior frontal areas, superior temporal areas, most of the parietal lobe

  • Typically caused by occlusion (thrombotic stroke) at the left middle cerebral artery (Alexander et al. 1992)

Lateralization of language functions



What does lateralized mean?



  • Many human cognitive functions are lateralized: brain shows functional asymmetries (one hemisphere is specialized in processing a given type of information)
  • Some cognitive functions are more lateralized than others, the origin of this lateralization is unclear
  • Cerebral hemispheres are interconnected by the corpus callosum: largest fiber system in the brain.

Corpus callosum



  • Connect correspondent brain areas in the two hemispheres (homotopic connection), but also non-correspondent areas (heterotopic connections)
  • Main function: inhibitory role; it prevents competition between brain hemispheres in information processing.

Corpus callosum


  • Corpus callosum matures slowly over the first 5 to 10 years of human life
  • Resection of corpus callosum or hemispherectomy in case of severe epilepsy in children under this age does not compromise the brain
  • Brain rewires itself!

Wada-Milner or Wada’s test



  • Injection of anesthetic agent into the internal carotid artery to anesthetize one hemisphere at a time
  • Used to determine cerebral dominance for language functions invasive and risky
  • Use for research now replaced with safer techniques
  • Still used with some patients in need of brain surgery: a patient’s first person perspective of Wada’s test!

Lateralization of language functions


For many years the preferred hand was believed to be contralateral to the cerebral hemisphere dominant for language:

  • Right-handers → left-hemisphere dominance
  • Left-handers → right-hemisphere dominance

Now: brain lateralization is only partially associated with handedness.

  • Right-handers → 95% have left-hemisphere dominance for language
  • Left-handers → 50-70% also show left-hemisphere dominance

Hand preference is relevant but not totally reliable as predictor of language dominance in the brain.

The auditory pathway


The auditory pathway transforms sound (air vibrations) into electric potentials, and transmits sensory information from the ear to the brain, where sophisticated speech processing is performed.


  1. Auditory nerve
  2. Primary auditory cortex
  3. Associative areas

The auditory pathway


1) Auditory nerve (cochlear nerve)

Tonotopic arrangement of sensors: from higher pitch to lower pitch, neurons located at the base and the apex of the cochlea, respectively.

The auditory pathway


2) Primary auditory cortex

  • Responds equally to speech and other sounds in both left and right hemisphere.
  • Anterior regions of left auditory cortex responds more to speech than non-speech sounds with same acoustic complexity (Scott et al. 2000)
  • Right hemisphere does not show this preference, suggesting specialization of left hemisphere for speech processing.

Left hemisphere damages can result in a type of auditory agnosia: environmental sounds and music are identified, but not speech

Research methods in infancy



  • Tasks that do not require verbal instructions or key-pressing responses
  • Short attention span
  • Get tired quickly
  • Experiments have to be fast (~5 minutes max)

Research with infants is challenging!

Research methods in infancy



  • Behavioral (based on infants’ natural tendency to look at conspicuous/novel visual stimuli)
  • Physiological (cardiac rhythm)
  • Brain imaging (EEG, fMRI, MEG, fNIRS)

Behavioural methods



Change in behavioral response = Ability to discriminate between two events

Behavioural methods



Headturn Preference Procedure (HPP) (Nelson et al. 1995)

  • Familiarization with acoustic stimuli (e.g., sequence of sounds)
  • Then, 2 types of audio stimuli
  • Presented while a flashing light alternates on the lateral screens
  • Infant would make head turns towards the light, and listen to the sounds as long as she wants
  • Sound stops when baby stops looking

Behavioural methods



Preferential Looking paradigm (Golinkoff et al. 1987)

  • Two images on a screen
  • Recording looking time at each image
  • Determines if infants can discriminate one stimulus from another by measuring how much time they look at them
  • Images can be paired with sounds to measure acoustic preferences through visual preferences

Behavioural methods



Preferential looking (eye-tracking)

  • Automated recording of infant’s eye-movements (eye-position, fixations, saccades..)
  • Based on infrared light (non-invasive) that create aa corneal reflection to locate eye position
  • Pupillometry

Behavioural methods



Habituation / Dishabituation

  • Showing the same stimulus repeatedly until infant shows a clear decrease in looking time
  • When a novel stimulus is presented, an increase in looking time should occur

Behavioural methods



High-Amplitude Sucking Procedure (Siqueland and DeLucia 1969)

Newborns is given a special pacifier that measures sucking rate

  • Newborn’s sucking frequency controls the presentation of sounds until bored (habituation)
  • Presentation of new sound: if newborn recognizes the change, she will increases the sucking rate

Neuroimaging methods



A change in neurophysiological response contingent to different types of stimulation may reveal differences in underlying procecesing between stimulus types.

Neuroimaging methods



Magnetoencephalography (MEG)

  • Records magnetic fields caused by electrical activity naturally produced in the brain
  • Good at spatially localize brain activation
  • Pros: baby is free to move; good spatial resolution
  • Cons: huge, very expensive

Neuroimaging methods



Functional Magnetic Resonance (fMRI)

  • Measures brain activity by detecting hemodynamic response in the brain (changes in blood flow)
  • Cons: lot of noise, babies should move as less as possible as movements can cause artifacts (distortions of images)

Neuroimaging methods



Electroencephalography (EEG)

Records electrical activity naturally produced by the brain

  • Measures voltage fluctuations resulting from ionic current within neurons (ERPs) in response to stimuli (images, sounds etc)
  • Pros: cheaper; more baby-friendly than other techniques; good to measure timecourse of activation
  • Cons: also sensitive to motion

Neuroimaging methods



Functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS)

Measures hemodynamic responses in the brain during stimuli presentation (images, sounds, etc.).

  • Infrared light goes into the scalp and comes back
  • Provides info about brain activation as amount of oxygen delivered by cerebral blood flow in a brain area
  • Pros: less invasive, less sensors
  • Cons: similar to fMRI

Preference for speech



At birth:

  • Humans show a preference for native spoken language vs. acoustically similar, non-language stimuli (e.g., complex sequence of non-linguistic sounds) since birth
  • Preference for the vocalizations emitted by conspecifics is a common phenomenon in the animal kingdom
  • For the young of different species, including infants, their own- species sounds (e.g., speech) have a “survival” importance

Preference for speech



At birth:

  • Newborns prefer their mother’s voice to other female voices (DeCasper et al. 1994)
  • Newborns stories heard prenatally from other stories (DeCasper and Spence 1986)
  • Native speech vs. non-native speech (Shultz and Vouloumanos 2010)

Phonotactics at birth?



2-to-5-day-old neonates (Gómez et al. 2014):

  • Linguistic universal: blif (well-formed syllable) vs. lbif (ill-formed syllable)
  • Left temporal and right fronto-parietal areas show larger haemodynamic responses when well-formed syllables are presented
  • fNIRS measures differences in blood oxygen level in these areas in response to well-formed (possible) syllables vs. ill-formed (impossible) syllables

Some evidence of language lateralization at birth (Peña et al. 2003):

  • Left temporal region responds differently to forward speech vs. backward speech vs. silence
  • Right temporal region does not

Prenatal experience with speech



Fetuses do not experience speech as we do: only low-frequency components of speech can be heard (e.g., pitch, rhythm, some phonetic information) (Moon 2017; Eggermont and Moore 2011; Gerhardt and Abrams 2000).

Fetuses have access to the mother’s voice mainly through bone conduction:

  • Mother’s voice is carried along the vertebral column into her pelvis to the bones of fetus’ head, then directly to fetus’ inner ear
  • At ~36th weeks of gestation, fetal heart rate (FHR) decelerates and motor activity decreases when hearing sound of mother’s voice

Prenatal experience with speech



Research on fetal sound discrimination confirms the ability of fetuses to detect and respond to maternal voice (May et al. 2011):

Mother’s voice seems to be a salient auditory stimulus even before birth, and has calming effect in fetuses

  • Effects on post-natal auditory preferences and behavior
  • Pre-natal experience can be one explanation of post-natal preferences for mother’s voice, and for speech
